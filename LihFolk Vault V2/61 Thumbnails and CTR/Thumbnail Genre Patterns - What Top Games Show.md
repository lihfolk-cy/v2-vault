Every Roblox genre has a visual dialect on Home: players learn what a simulator tile, a horror tile or a steal-game tile looks like, and they use that dialect to sort the row in a glance. This note describes those recurring patterns genre by genre, as observed on public top-game listings in October 2026, and how to use them: speak the dialect so the right players recognise your genre, then add one twist so your tile is the one they pick. It describes patterns, not specific images. For a dated, game-by-game snapshot of titles, icons and thumbnails see [[Cross-Game Patterns - Thumbnails Icons and Titles That Get Clicks]]; for the design rules underneath see [[Thumbnail Composition for High CTR - Focal Point, Contrast and Readability]]; for the genres themselves see [[Genre Playbook - Simulators]], [[Genre Playbook - Tycoons]], [[Genre Playbook - Obbies and Platformers]], [[Genre Playbook - Horror and Story]], [[Genre Playbook - Battlegrounds and Arena Fighters]], [[Genre Playbook - Roleplay and Social Hangouts]] and [[Genre Playbook - RNG and Collection Games]]. Hub: [[Thumbnails and CTR Start Here]].

Labels: patterns are **Rule of thumb** observations (no one outside a studio can see another game's qPTR, so none of these is proven to cause clicks); platform facts are **Roblox docs**.

## 0. How to read a genre's dialect yourself

1. Open Home, Discover charts and Search for your genre's main verb on a phone. Screenshot the first 20 tiles (for your own study only; do not reuse others' art).
2. Paste them into a grid at the same size. Note for each: focal subject, emotion, dominant two colours, text (if any), scale contrast, and what the promise is in one phrase.
3. Count. If 15 of 20 are yellow-green with a grinning avatar and a big number, that is the dialect.
4. Decide your **recognition cues** (two or three things you keep so your genre reads) and your **difference cue** (one thing you change so you stand out).

Repeat monthly; dialects drift with trends ([[Competitor Analysis Method]]).

## 1. Simulators (click, upgrade, rebirth)

**Typical tile:** a grinning or shocked avatar front and centre, surrounded by an explosion of collectibles (coins, gems, pets), often a huge multiplier or stat, saturated rainbow palette, sky-blue or bright-green backgrounds.

**Why it works:** the dream is **growth**. The image sells "you will be enormously more powerful than now".

**Recognition cues to keep:** a happy, excited avatar; a visible currency; bright daylight palette.

**Ways to stand out:**
- Show **before-and-after scale** in one frame (a tiny starter avatar and a giant late-game version).
- Replace the generic coin explosion with **your one unique collectible** at hero size.
- Use a cooler or night palette in a row of noon-bright tiles, if your game has a matching zone.

**Truth trap:** numbers bigger than any player can reach early. If you show a late-game stat, it must be real in the game.

## 2. Tycoons and idle builders

**Typical tile:** an isometric or high-angle view of a dense, glowing base or factory, a grinning owner avatar in the foreground, money text or a per-second income figure, warm gold and green.

**Why it works:** the dream is **ownership**: "this could be yours".

**Ways to stand out:**
- Show the base at **dusk with lights on**; lit windows and neon read better than daytime detail at tile size.
- Put a **specific, odd** building at hero size (a rocket launchpad, a candy factory) instead of a generic sprawl.
- If income is the dream, make the number the hero and keep the base as a silhouette behind it (the "number art" strategy described in the cross-game note).

**Truth trap:** fully upgraded bases that take weeks to build shown as if they were the start. Show a mid-game base or caption nothing.

---

## 3. Obbies and platformers

**Typical tile:** a tall, colourful tower or path from a low angle, a tiny avatar mid-jump or mid-fall, lava or void below, bright primary colours, sometimes a stage count.

**Why it works:** the dream is **challenge and mastery**, plus the comedy of falling.

**Ways to stand out:**
- **Mid-fall panic** reads instantly: a flailing avatar with an open-mouth face just above lava.
- Show **one memorable obstacle** large (a giant swinging hammer, a moving keyboard) rather than the whole course.
- For themed obbies, the theme object is the hero (a giant cake, a giant phone).

**Truth trap:** obstacles that do not exist, or a tower taller than the real one.

See [[Teardown - Tower of Hell]] for how one of the genre's anchors presents the tower itself as the promise.

## 4. Horror and chase

**Typical tile:** dark frame, a single monster or entity with bright eyes or a glowing feature, one or two terrified avatars small in the foreground, desaturated palette with one accent colour (red, sickly green, cold blue), heavy vignette, little or no text.

**Why it works:** the dream is **fear with friends**. Docs explicitly suggest heavy contrast for horror (**Roblox docs**, https://create.roblox.com/docs/production/promotion/game-icons).

**Ways to stand out:**
- **Eyes are the focal point.** In a row of dark tiles, the brightest pair of eyes wins attention.
- Use **an unusual accent colour** for your entity (gold, magenta) in a genre of red and green.
- Show the **situation**, not just the monster: a door ajar, a corridor with the entity at the end, a hiding spot. The situation tells the mechanic.
- Funny-scary horror (party horror for younger players) can use a brighter palette and a goofy monster; match your real tone.

**Truth trap:** gore or realism beyond the maturity label and beyond the game's art. Thumbnails must fit your experience's rating ([[Roblox Policies Ratings and Compliance]]).

## 5. Anime fighting, battlegrounds and RPG combat

**Typical tile:** one or two characters mid-attack, huge energy VFX (slashes, beams, auras) filling half the frame, dynamic diagonal composition, saturated high-contrast palette (purple, cyan, gold, red), speed lines, sometimes a move or character name.

**Why it works:** the dream is **power fantasy**: "this move could be yours".

**Ways to stand out:**
- **One signature move** at hero size beats a lineup of characters.
- Two fighters **clashing** at the centre (opposing colours meeting) gives built-in contrast and story.
- A **close-up of the eyes and the weapon** works as a variant for players who already know the genre.

**Truth trap:** VFX more elaborate than the game's. Freeze your **real** effect at its most dramatic frame instead; see [[Making Thumbnails in Studio and Blender - Capture, Lighting and Posing]]. See [[Teardown - Blade Ball]] and [[Teardown - Jujutsu Shenanigans]] for combat presentation in practice.

## 6. Roleplay, social hangouts and life sims

**Typical tile:** a group of well-dressed avatars posing together in front of an appealing house, school, city street or café; warm daylight or golden hour; friendly expressions; logo-led branding; very little text.

**Why it works:** the dream is **belonging and self-expression**: friends, outfits, a place.

**Ways to stand out:**
- Show **an activity**, not just a pose: a group around a car, a sleepover, a job uniform. The activity is the hook.
- Diverse, fashionable avatars matter more here than anywhere; dated outfits make the game look abandoned.
- Seasonal variants are expected in this genre (see [[Live Ops Events and Updates]]).

**Truth trap:** houses, vehicles or locations that are not in the game, or paid content shown as if free (it can be shown, but do not imply it is free).

See [[Teardown - Welcome to Bloxburg]] for a long-running life-sim's approach.

## 7. RNG and collection games

**Typical tile:** a glowing rarity reveal (an aura, a sword, a pet) at hero size, an amazed avatar, rarity colours (gold, rainbow, black-and-red "secret"), sometimes odds text like "1 in X".

**Why it works:** the dream is **luck**: "the next roll could be this".

**Ways to stand out:**
- Make the reveal moment the hero: the item emerging, particles bursting, the avatar reacting.
- Use your **real rarity banner** from the UI as the text.
- If you show odds, they must match your real odds table ([[Paid Random Items and Odds Disclosure in Practice]]).

See [[Teardown - Sol's RNG]].

## 8. Trend games: brainrot, steal-a-X and similar

**Typical tile:** a bright, chaotic scene with recognisable meme-style characters, an avatar sprinting away with a stolen prize, a chasing threat or rival, a huge per-second income figure, very saturated colours, and a title of verb plus noun.

**Why it works:** the dream is **the loop in one frame**: grab, run, earn. The meme characters signal "this is the trend you have heard about".

**Ways to stand out:**
- Your **twist must be visible**. If your game is "steal, but on a tsunami" or "steal, but eggs", the twist object is the hero, not the generic loot.
- Show the **danger and the goal together**: the thief, the prize and the pursuer in a single diagonal ([[Teardown - Escape Tsunami for Brainrots]], [[Teardown - Steal An Egg]]).
- Trends move fast; plan art you can re-skin in a day ([[Viral Game Anatomy - Hooks, Loops and Timing]]).

**Truth trap:** using characters or brands you do not have rights to, or another game's mascots. Use your own characters; original creatures in the trend's style read just as well. Also avoid duplicating another game's thumbnail layout closely: the Community Standards prohibit duplicative or misleading metadata (https://about.roblox.com/community-standards).

See [[Case Study - Steal a Brainrot]] and [[Viral and Trend Teardowns Start Here]].

## 9. Patterns that cut across genres

| Pattern | Seen in | Why it helps | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Face in the upper third looking at the prize or viewer | Most genres | Faces are found first | Default faces are small; get closer |
| Small avatar, huge thing | Simulators, horror, obby, trend | Instant scale and spectacle | Big thing must exist in the game |
| One accent colour against a calm field | Horror, high-craft games | Pops in a busy row | Accent must belong to the subject |
| Number as hero | Simulators, tycoons, trend | Sells growth directly | Never in the bottom strip; must be reachable |
| Platform or seasonal frame on a duplicate of the main art | Large games during events | Personalisation picks framed vs clean | Remove when the event ends |
| Logo top-left or top-centre | Established brands | Recognition for returning players | New games gain more from the promise than the logo |

High-craft studios tend to use fewer, cleaner elements and consistent art direction across years ([[What Craft Looks Like on Roblox - Lessons from Top Studios]], [[High-Craft Teardowns Start Here]]); trend games tend toward maximum saturation and motion. Both read at tile size because both keep one clear subject.

## Common mistakes

- Avoid: copying the genre leader's tile closely. Symptom: players assume you are a clone, and the leader wins the comparison.
- Avoid: ignoring the dialect entirely. Symptom: a beautiful tile that players cannot place in a genre, so they skip it.
- Avoid: a horror palette on a cheerful game (or the reverse). Symptom: wrong audience clicks, high first-play bounce.
- Avoid: meme characters you do not own. Symptom: moderation and rights trouble.
- Avoid: one generic tile for a game with a clear twist. Symptom: no reason to pick you over the five similar games in the row.

## Checklist

- [ ] 20 genre tiles studied at phone size; dialect written down.
- [ ] Two or three recognition cues kept; one difference cue chosen.
- [ ] The twist or unique object is visible at hero size.
- [ ] Every element on the tile exists in the game at the stage shown.
- [ ] Original characters only; no other game's mascots or layout.
- [ ] Palette and tone match the real game and its maturity label.
- [ ] Dialect re-checked monthly while the genre is trending.
